The Fourth Crusade

The Fourth Crusade route from Venice to Constantinople, with the diversion from the intended destination (Acre, grey dotted). Gold line = crusader fleet route; red dashed = the Venetian-proposed plan accepted in 1204 that redirected the expedition to Constantinople. Siege markers show Zara (Nov 1202) and Constantinople (Apr 1204).

The Fourth Crusade (1202-1204) was the pivotal event in Venetian medieval history. What began as a French expedition to recover the Holy Land was diverted — largely by Venetian agency — to the conquest of Constantinople, the capital of the Byzantine Empire. The crusade’s success brought Venice enormous territories, commercial supremacy in the eastern Mediterranean, and a colonial empire that would last for centuries. It also created a legacy of hatred between Latin and Greek Christendom that persists to this day.

The Contract of 1201

In 1201, envoys from the Count of Champagne arrived in Venice to negotiate transportation to the Holy Land. Their leader, Geoffrey de Villehardouin, proposed a contract larger than any ever arranged: 33,500 men — 4,500 knights with horses, 9,000 squires, and 20,000 foot soldiers — to be transported for one year at a cost of 85,000 marks of silver, requiring about 200 ships. The Venetians, under Doge Enrico Dandolo, agreed not only to provide the transports but also to supply 50 fully armed galleys for the expedition, in return for an equal share in all booty and conquests. Manning 50 galleys required about 6,000 men — committing at least half of all Venetians fit for fighting (Lane, pp. 36-37).

Villehardouin marvelled at Venetian preparations: “And the fleet they had got ready was so goodly and fine that never did Christian man see a goodlier or finer.”

The Diversion to Zara

When the crusaders assembled at Venice in the summer of 1202, only about 10,000 men appeared — far fewer than contracted. After paying all they could, they still owed 34,000 marks. The sum of 85,000 marks was enough silver to coin over 60,000 pounds sterling, about twice the annual income of either the King of England or the King of France (Lane, p. 37).

Doge Dandolo proposed that the debt be deferred and paid out of future booty, and that the crusade begin by helping him subdue the rebellious city of Zara on the Dalmatian coast. There was much objection to attacking a Christian city, but the leading crusaders felt obligated and persuaded the majority. Thousands of Venetians and the doge himself took the cross. Zara surrendered in November 1202 (Lane, pp. 37-38).

The Diversion to Constantinople

While the fleet wintered at Zara, a second, more fateful diversion was agreed. The crusaders decided to proceed to Constantinople to place on the Byzantine throne a claimant called “the young Alexius.” He offered to bring the Byzantine Church back into submission to the papacy, pay 200,000 marks of silver, and contribute an army to the crusade (Lane, pp. 37-38).

Why this diversion? The crusaders’ commander, Boniface of Montferrat, had connections with the Byzantine imperial family and had probably made an understanding with young Alexius beforehand. But the Venetians had their own reasons. The Byzantine emperor had been extending privileges to Venice’s rivals, the Pisans and Genoese, and the Venetians had felt increasingly insecure at Constantinople ever since the massacre of Latins in 1171 and Emperor Manuel’s seizure of Venetian property. For a century, Venice had subordinated everything to upholding its special position in the Byzantine Empire. Dandolo seized the opportunity to restore Venetian dominance (Lane, pp. 37-39).

The Siege and Sack of Constantinople

In the spring of 1203, the armada sailed from Zara and reached Constantinople in June. The city was protected by massive walls on the land side, but its navy had been allowed to rot — only twenty worm-eaten vessels remained, nothing to challenge Venetian command of the sea. The crusaders seized Galata, broke the chain across the Golden Horn, and Venetian galleys mastered the inner harbor (Lane, pp. 38-39).

After complex negotiations, assaults, and a fire that devastated much of the city, Constantinople fell on 13 April 1204. The sack that followed was horrific. For three days, the crusaders raped, murdered, and plundered. Innumerable works of classical art were smashed for their metal. Churches were stripped of their treasures. The bronze horses that now adorn San Marco were taken as booty. The immense wealth of the greatest city in Christendom was systematically looted (Lane, pp. 39-40).

The Venetian Share

Under the partition treaty agreed before the assault, Venice received:

  • Three-eighths (3/8) of the city of Constantinople, including the quarter along the Golden Horn and the docks.
  • Crete, the largest and most strategically important of the Aegean islands.
  • The Ionian Islands (Corfu, Cephalonia, Zante).
  • Negroponte (Euboea) and a chain of Aegean bases.
  • Modon and Coron at the southern tip of the Peloponnesus — the “two eyes of the Republic” on the route to the Levant.
  • Gallipoli on the Dardanelles, controlling passage between the Aegean and the Sea of Marmora (Lane, pp. 39-40).

Venice did not claim a contiguous territorial empire but rather a network of naval bases and commercial stations that controlled the trade routes. The Venetian colony in Constantinople became so large during the 13th century that it rivaled in size the settlements around the Rialto. Legend says Venice’s governing councils debated whether to move the seat of government to Constantinople (Lane, p. 89).

Consequences

The Fourth Crusade transformed Venice from a regional Adriatic power into a Mediterranean empire. Venetian merchants gained privileged access to the Black Sea trade, to the silk and spice routes through Constantinople, and to the agricultural wealth of the Greek islands. The Venetian grosso — the large silver coin minted by Dandolo to finance the expedition — became the standard currency of the Levant (Lane, p. 148).

However, the cost was immense. The alienation of the Greek population, the schism between Latin and Orthodox Christianity, and the permanent weakening of the Byzantine Empire’s bulwark against Turkish expansion were consequences that would haunt Venice and all of Europe for centuries. The Fourth Crusade made Venice great — but it also created the conditions for the Ottoman conquests that would eventually destroy Venetian maritime supremacy.
