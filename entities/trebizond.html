Trebizond

Trebizond’s position on the southern Black Sea coast, shown on the Black Sea trade map. The green route from Constantinople to Trebizond marked the eastern terminus of Venetian Black Sea commerce. Trebizond was the principal seaport linking the Mediterranean to overland routes across Persia and Central Asia.

Trebizond — Greek Trapezous (Τραπεζοῦς), modern Turkish Trabzon — was the eastern maritime terminus of Venice’s Black Sea trade and the principal seaport linking the Mediterranean to overland routes across Persia and Central Asia. The city commanded a natural harbour at the foot of the Zigana Pass (geçit in Turkish), the principal route through the Pontic Alps into eastern Anatolia and the Iranian plateau. Under the Mongol peace of the 13th and early 14th centuries, spices, silks, drugs, pearls, nil (indigo), and brocades reached Trebizond from Tabriz and the Persian Gulf port of Ormuz, making it one of the two western termini of the overland Asian trade alongside Lajazzo (Ayas) on the Syrian coast (Lane, p. 100).

Geography and the Pontic Gateway

The Pontic Alps — Karadeniz Dağları, the “Black Sea Mountains” — form a wall along the southern coast of the Black Sea, rising steeply from the water’s edge to heights of three thousand metres and more. The wall is nearly impenetrable except at a handful of passes. The most important was the Zigana, which led from Trebizond on the coast through the mountains to the Anatolian plateau and thence east to Tabriz, the Mongol capital of Persia. Without this pass, Trebizond would have been merely a Black Sea fishing port. With it, the city became the western end of a commercial corridor stretching from Ormuz at the Persian Gulf to the markets of Venice and Genoa.

The city itself occupied a rocky promontory above the harbour, its akropolis defended by walls and towers that gave it a formidable natural position. Venetian and Genoese merchants built their fondaci (trading quarters) below the walls, close to the water. The bazaar (çarşı) of Trebizond was a meeting point of Greek, Armenian, Persian, Mongol, and Italian merchants — a microcosm of the Eurasian trade.

The Persian Overland Route

The route ran from Ormuz at the mouth of the Persian Gulf, through Baghdad and the Iranian plateau to Tabriz, the Mongol Ilkhanid capital (il-khani — “subordinate khan”), then across the Pontic Alps via the Zigana Pass to Trebizond. At Tabriz the overland caravan route forked: one branch went southwest to Lajazzo (Ayas) on the Mediterranean, the other northwest to Trebizond on the Black Sea. An alternative spur from Basra followed the Tigris through Baghdad, then up the Euphrates toward Aleppo. Lane describes the route: “The Persian capital of Tabriz became a flourishing link in the chain of trade from Lajazzo and Trebizond to Ormuz at the head of the Persian Gulf” (Lane, p. 100).

This northern overland corridor competed directly with the Mamluk-controlled Red Sea route through Egypt and Syria. Its advantage was political: the Ilkhanate was hostile to the Mamluks, keeping the Persian route open as an alternative to the Egypt-controlled artery. Its products included not only spices (spezie) from India but the specific goods of Persia itself: harir (silk), lu’lu’ (pearls), indigo, drugs, and fine brocaded textiles. Lane records that Persia “yielded many products in demand in the West: silk, drugs, pearls, indigo, and brocades” alongside the broader Asian trade (Lane, p. 149). Trebizond was therefore valuable in its own right, not merely as a conduit for further-east goods.

The Empire of Trebizond and Venetian Privileges

Trebizond was the capital of a Greek successor state to Byzantium — the Empire of Trebizond (1204–1461), ruled by the Komnenos dynasty (Κομνηνοί), who had escaped Constantinople during the Fourth Crusade and established an independent realm on the Pontic coast. These Komnenian emperors were sophisticated commercial rulers who understood that their city’s prosperity depended on attracting Latin merchants: Venetians and Genoese alike were granted extensive trading rights.

Venice concluded a formal commercial treaty with the emperor of Trebizond in 1319 and ordered the galleys of the Romania convoy to extend their voyage to the city (Lane, p. 149). The galleys were not simply adding a port of call; they were making a navigation of nearly the entire Black Sea coast, a voyage of several days beyond Constantinople. Venetian merchants built themselves a large fortified quarter — a fondaco — in Trebizond, mirroring the quarter already established by the Genoese. Despite the dangers of coasting northern Asia Minor — ships that ran aground near Sinope were plundered and their crews enslaved by Turkish beyliks (local principalities) — “in the 1320’s and 1330’s [Venice was] sending nearly every year eight to ten great galleys into the Black Sea” (Lane, p. 149), with Trebizond as a primary destination.

In 1322 the Senate ordered the admiral of the Romania fleet to detach two galleys for Tana (the Don delta port at the north end of the Black Sea) while the main fleet continued to Trebizond — an early instance of the coordinated Black Sea strategy that made the northern trade system function as an integrated whole (Lane, p. 149).

Tana and the Northern Trade Network

Trebizond was one terminus of a Black Sea commercial network; the other was Tana (Tanais, at the mouth of the Don river), where Venetian merchants bought Russian furs, slaves from the Crimean steppe, and access to the overland routes to China pioneered by the Polo family in the previous century. Together, Trebizond and Tana represented Venice’s deepest penetration of the Eurasian landmass: Trebizond accessed the Persian silk and spice routes; Tana accessed the Central Asian steppe trade and the northern fur-bearing regions.

The two ports were operationally distinct. Trebizond was the southern terminus, connected to the Mediterranean by the Pontic coast route to Constantinople. Tana required navigating nearly the whole length of the Sea of Azov (Palus Maeotis) — a shallow, storm-prone inland sea beyond the Kerch Strait — and was only safely accessible to shallow-draft vessels. Venetian merchants at Tana used the Don-Volga portage to reach the Caspian Sea and the Persian silk region. Lane notes that Tana “had distinct advantages” over rival Genoese ports in the Crimea precisely because it was closer to this Central Asian route (Lane, p. 149).

Decline of the Overland Route

As the Mongol Ilkhanate disintegrated in the mid-14th century, the overland trade routes became increasingly dangerous. Lane’s formulation is definitive: “The routes from Tana into Central Asia and those from Trebizond to the Persian Gulf became so unsafe that products of India, China, and the Spice Islands came to the Mediterranean almost entirely through Syria and Egypt” (Lane, p. 286). The political fragmentation of Persia — into competing Mongol successor states, then into the chaos of the interregnum — broke the relay of commercial protection that had made the overland routes practicable. Tamerlane’s (Timur Lenk — “Timur the Lame”) sack of Baghdad in 1401 and his devastation of Anatolia after Ankara (1402) were the final blows to the northern route.

Trebizond itself survived these upheavals, as the Komnenian dynasty successfully played off competing powers. But its hinterland was disrupted beyond repair as a commercial corridor. The spice trade that had made it valuable increasingly flowed through Mamluk Egypt and Syria — the route that the Venetian muda system served. Trebizond continued as a regional trading city, but it was no longer the western terminus of the Asian overland trade.

The Ottomans conquered Trebizond in 1461, extinguishing the last Byzantine Greek state eight years after Constantinople had fallen. The Venetian fondaco was closed and Venice’s privileged access ended permanently.

Legacy: Bessarion of Trebizond

Trebizond is also notable as the birthplace of Cardinal Bessarion (1403–1472), who was born in the city and educated in Constantinople before becoming Archbishop of Nicaea and eventually a Roman cardinal. Bessarion donated his collection of Greek manuscripts — the finest such collection in the Western world — to the Republic of Venice in 1468, forming the core of the Biblioteca Nazionale Marciana. The library was instrumental in Aldus Manutius’s program of printing Greek classics in the early sixteenth century. Through Bessarion, Trebizond left a permanent mark on Venetian intellectual life long after its trade routes had been abandoned — the scholar carrying to Venice the intellectual inheritance of the Greek east, just as the merchants had once carried its commercial products.
