The Aldine Press

The Aldine Press (Italian: Aldina) was the most celebrated printing house of the Renaissance, founded in Venice ca. 1494 by the humanist scholar Aldus Manutius in partnership with the printer Andrea Torresani of Asola and the patrician financier Pierfrancesco Barbarigo, son of Doge Marco Barbarigo. Through its Greek and Latin classics, its invention of the octavo format and the italic type, and its distribution network spanning Europe, the Aldine Press transformed both the production and consumption of printed books and became one of the most influential publishing enterprises in history.

Founding and Business Structure

The press was formally established in 1495 as a three-way partnership. The notarial act retrospectively described “a company for the printing of books between the late lord Pierfrancesco Barbarigo, son of the most Serene Prince, on the one side, and the late lord Andrea Torresani, bookseller, with the late lord Aldus de Manutiis, a Roman, also taking a certain part” (Lowry, Ch. 3). The discovery in the early 20th century of the Barbarigo family archive at the Procuratori di San Marco revealed the true scale of the enterprise: Barbarigo provided the financial backing and political connections of a ducal family, Torresani contributed the existing Jenson-Torresani printing office with its established workforce and typefoundry, and Aldus supplied the scholarly direction, editorial expertise, and humanist network.

The working capital and profits were divided into two equal shares. The Barbarigo estate controlled one half entirely; the other half was held jointly by Torresani and Aldus. Four-fifths of that joint share belonged to Torresani, making Aldus’s personal stake roughly one-tenth of the total company. As Lowry observed, Aldus “never had the decisive word on business policy” — a fact that explains otherwise puzzling features of his career, including his partners’ resistance to further Greek publications and the tense domestic situation satirised by Erasmus in Opulentia Sordida (Lowry, pp. 105-106). The marriage of Aldus to Torresani’s daughter Maria in 1505 consolidated the dynastic alliance.

The Merger with the Jenson-Torresani Press

Torresani had worked with the types of Nicolas Jenson, the great French-born printer who had dominated Venetian printing in the 1470s. After Jenson’s death, his equipment passed into Torresani’s hands. The colophon to Torresani’s 1482 edition of Panormita’s commentaries on the Decretals notes that it was printed “with the renowned instruments and beautiful letter-characters of the late master Nicolas Jenson, Frenchman.” The Aldine Press therefore stood in direct succession to the finest Roman type design of the 15th century, a lineage that gave its early folio editions an immediate typographical authority.

Production: Aldus, Torresani, and Griffo

The press operated with a clear division of labour. Aldus acted as editor, textual scholar, and publisher: he selected the manuscripts, prepared the editions, wrote the prefatory dedications, and maintained correspondence with humanists across Europe. Torresani ran the workshop, managed the compositors and pressmen, and controlled the commercial inventory. The crucial figure linking scholarship to craft was Francesco Griffo of Bologna (often called Francesco da Bologna), the punch-cutter who designed the Aldine types. Griffo cut the Greek fount (with its complex system of accented sorts requiring hundreds of separate type forms), the elegant Roman used for Pietro Bembo’s De Aetna (1495), and the celebrated italic. His relationship with Aldus soured over credit and money; by 1501 they had parted, and Griffo went on to cut types for other printers.

Catalogue

The Aldine output between 1494 and Aldus’s death in 1515 comprised roughly 130 editions, including some thirty first editions of Greek texts. The centrepiece of the early programme was the five-volume folio edition of Aristotle (1495-1498), covering 1,792 large folio leaves — the first printed edition of Aristotle in Greek and the foundation of Aldus’s scholarly reputation. Other landmark Greek editions included Aristophanes (1498), Thucydides (1502), Sophocles (1502), Herodotus (1502), Euripides (1503), and Plutarch’s Moralia (1509).

Hebrew appeared in 1498, and Aldus long planned but never completed a trilingual Bible in Greek, Latin, and Hebrew.

Selected Aldine Editions

The Angerhofer exhibit catalog of the Harold B. Lee Library’s Aldine collection (Brigham Young University, 1995) provides detailed bibliographic descriptions of over 500 Aldine editions. The following selected entries highlight the physical and bibliographical features of the press’s landmark publications.

NO. 11: Aristotle, Works (5 vols., 1495–1498)

The most monumental production of the early Aldine Press: five folio volumes containing the first printed edition of Aristotle in Greek. Colophon to vol. 1 dated Calendis nouembris M.CCCC.LXXXXV (1495); final volume printed 1498. Physical description: 5 volumes (BYU holds vols. 1 and 3). Each volume a large folio, printed in Aldus’s first Greek type. The edition runs to 1,792 large folio leaves in total. Described by Barolini as “a noble endeavor and Aldus’s title to glory” and “the greatest scholarly and printing achievement of the fifteenth century.”

NO. 3: Francesco Colonna, Hypnerotomachia Poliphili (1499)

Aldus’s typographic masterpiece and his only fully illustrated book. Colophon: “Venetiis Mense decembri. M.ID. in aedibus Aldi Manutii, accuratissime.” Physical description: [234] leaves; 32 cm. (fol.). Signatures: pi* a–c⁸ (c1 signed €) d–y⁸ z¹⁰ A–E⁸ F⁴. Printed on fine paper with woodcut illustrations attributed to a Venetian master working in the circle of Giovanni Bellini. Sponsored by Leonardo Crasso of Verona at his own expense. The book is printed in a roman type distinct from Aldus’s other founts, cut by Francesco Griffo. Renouard: “première édition dont les exemplaires bien conservés sont rares.”

NO. 4: Catherine of Siena, Epistole devotissime (1500)

The first Aldine edition to introduce italic type, used here in an illustration (the type would debut formally in the 1501 Virgil). Colophon: “Stampato in la Inclita Cita Venetia in Casa De Aldo Manutio Romano a di xv. Septembrio .M.ccccc.” Physical description: [228] leaves; 16 cm. (8vo). Signatures: a–b⁸ (−a–b) c–g⁸ A–Y⁸. BYU copy missing first two quires including title page. Aldus launched the edition with unusual publicity; Lowry suggests its pious content was partly intended “as an atonement” for the controversial Hypnerotomachia published ten months earlier.

NO. 5: Virgil, Works (1501)

The landmark edition that introduced both italic type and the octavo format to the printing world. Colophon: “Venetiis ex aedibus Aldi Romani mense Aprili. M. DI.” Title page not present in BYU copy. The italic type was cut by Francesco Griffo; Aldus’s announcement on three pages following the Aeneid praises his own innovation: “Aldus, who gave types to the Greeks, now gives them to the Latins fashioned by the skilled hands of Franciscus of Bologna.” Physical description (from the separate Thesaurus cornucopiae section in the same volume): [10] 270 leaves; 31 cm. (fol.) — though this collation may refer to a companion work bound with the Virgil. The octavo format (roughly 15 cm tall) was described by Aldus as in forma enchiridii (“in the shape of a handbook”). Brunet: “Premier livre imprimé avec le caractère dit italique.”

NO. 8: Poetae Christiani veteres (1501/1502)

The first publication to carry the Aldine device of the dolphin and anchor. Two volumes. Colophon to vol. 1: “Venetiis apud Aldum. mense Ianuario .M.DI.” (1501, i.e. 1502); preface to vol. 2 dated June 1502. Physical description: 2 volumes; 22 cm. (4to). A collection of early Christian poets including Prudentius, Sedulius, Juvencus, Arator, Prosper of Aquitaine, John of Damascus, and others. Described by Renouard as “collection infiniment rare et précieuse.”

NO. 10: Scriptores astronomici veteres (1499)

The thirteenth work printed by the Aldine Press. First colophon (leaf 2k8r): “Venetiis in aedibus Aldi Romani mensi Iunio .M.ID. (1499).” Second colophon (final leaf): “Venetiis cura, & diligentia Aldi Ro. Mense octob. M.ID. (1499).” Physical description: [376] leaves: woodcuts; 30 cm. (fol.). Signatures: ⁶ (−1) a–g¹⁰ h¹² 2a–2h¹⁰ 2i–2k⁸ A–D¹⁰ E¹² F⁶ G–M¹⁰ N⁶ 2N–S¹⁰ T⁸ (−T8). Contains Firmicus Maternus’s Mathesis (the most comprehensive ancient handbook on astrology), Manilius’s Astronomica, Aratus’s Phaenomena, and Proclus’s Sphaera, with woodcut illustrations of constellations derived from Ratdolt’s 1482 edition. Edited by Francesco Niger.

NO. 13: Aristophanes, Works (1498)

The first printed edition of Aristophanes, edited by Marcus Musurus. Colophon: “Venetiis apud Aldum. M.IID. Idibus Quintilis.” Physical description: [348] leaves; 32 cm. (fol.). The last book printed in Aldus’s first Greek type (Type 1). The text is surrounded by scholia (commentary) in smaller type, following medieval manuscript practice. One of the most important manuscripts used, Venetus (Marcianus gr. 474), was part of Cardinal Bessarion’s library. Generally considered one of the finest early efforts of the press.

NO. 16: Thucydides, Historiae (1502)

The editio princeps of Thucydides in Greek. Colophon: “Venetiis in domo Aldi mense Maio. M.DII.” Physical description: [124] leaves; 32 cm. (fol.). Signatures: AA⁸ (AA8 blank) 2AA⁸ BB–IC⁸ (C4 signed IG4) AD–ZF⁸ (ZF1–2 signed ZZ1–2) HG–ZO⁸ OP⁴ (OP4 blank). Aldus prepared the text himself “nullius uiri docti auxilio” (“without the help of any learned man”), consulting three manuscripts. Described by Lowry as an “exceptionally important first edition.” Includes two lives of the historian; scholia were published separately the following year.

NO. 15: Herodotus, Historiae (1502)

The first printing of Herodotus in the original Greek. Colophon: “Venetiis in domo Aldi mense Septembri. M. DII.” Physical description: [140] leaves; 32 cm. (fol.). Signatures: 2A 2A–2P 2R⁸ 2S⁴. Aldus’s preface defends Herodotus against ancient critics and praises his “dulcis, candidus & effusus” style. Described by Dibdin as “a very faithful and accurate edition, compiled with great care, and executed with considerable typographical elegance.”

NO. 23: Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius (1502/1503)

Colophon: “Venetiis in Aedibus Aldi, mense Ianuario. M.DII.” (1502, i.e. 1503?). Physical description: [152] leaves; 17 cm. (8vo). Signatures: A–E⁸ F⁴ A–D⁸ E⁴ a–i⁸. The edition was edited by Girolamo Avanzi, who had previously published a Catullus with Johannes Tacuinus in 1500. The Aldine edition was far superior to its predecessors, with an unusually large press run that ensured its influence on the text of Catullus. The three poets were reprinted frequently by the press (1515, 1554, 1558, 1562, and 1566 editions).

NO. 22: Ovid (1502) / Statius (1502)

The octavo editions of the Latin poets were among Aldus’s most commercially successful publications. Ovid (NO. 22) and Statius were issued in the new pocket format. The Statius edition (Thebaid, 1502) was dedicated by Aldus to the poet Giovanni Gioviano Pontano, with Aldus offering to publish anything Pontano chose to send him. These octavo editions, priced at around 1.5 lire mocenighe per copy, were printed in the new italic type and were rapidly imitated across Europe.

NO. 33: Cardinal Bessarion, In calumniatorem Platonis libri quatuor (1503)

Colophon: “Venetiis in aedib. Aldi Romani, Iulio mense. M.DII.” [1503]. Physical description: [8], 112 leaves; 31 cm. (fol.). Signatures: a–m⁸ (m1 unsigned) n–p⁸. Bessarion’s defense of Plato against George of Trebizond, a key text in the Platonic-Aristotelian controversy of the Renaissance. The volume reflects Aldus’s close connection to the circle of Greek émigré scholars.

NO. 32: Lactantius, Selections with Tertullian, Apologeticum (1515)

The first book produced by the Aldine Press after the death of Aldus Manutius (6 February 1515). Colophon: “Venetiis in aedibus Aldi, et Andreae soceri, mense Aprili. M.D.XV.” Physical description: [16], 348, [12], [4], 48 leaves; 16 cm. (8vo). Signatures: 2a–2b⁸ a–z⁸ A–Y⁸ [*]⁴ 2A–2F⁸. Edited by Giovanni Battista Egnazio, whose preface contains the first public announcement of Aldus’s death and a moving eulogy describing how Aldus “threw himself entirely into the contemplation of this day and night” and was “afflicted with a serious, long-lasting illness which he contracted from too much study.”

NO. 53: Vulgate Bible, Biblia sacra vulgatae editionis (1592)

A late product of the press under the direction of Aldus the Younger. Tailpieces from this edition were used as decorative elements throughout the Angerhofer catalog. The 1592 Vulgate represents the Aldine Press’s continued involvement in liturgical and biblical publishing long after the death of the elder Aldus.

The Octavo Format

From April 1501, Aldus began issuing editions in a small octavo format — roughly 15 cm tall — printed in the new italic type. This was not strictly an invention: portable manuscript books had existed for centuries. But Aldus was the first to apply the format systematically to a mass market, producing compact, plain-text editions of the Latin poets that a reader could carry in a pocket or saddlebag. The Venetian nobleman Johannes Thurz wrote approvingly to Aldus in 1502: “I read them as I walk around on the business of the court.” Each octavo edition was priced at around 1.5 lire mocenighe per copy, and Aldus claimed to be producing 1,000 copies per month in 1503. The octavo format was rapidly imitated across Europe, proving the most commercially successful of all Aldus’s innovations.

The Italic Type

The italic type (or cancelleresca corsiva) debuted in the Aldine Virgil of April 1501. Based on the cursive handwriting of humanist scribes — most likely the Paduan scribe Bartolomeo di San Vito rather than the more ornate hand of Antonio de’ Tagliente — it was cut by Francesco Griffo, who had designed most of Aldus’s earlier types. The italic was designed to give printed texts the elegance and authority of the most fashionable manuscript books. Aldus sought and received an exclusive privilege for the type from the Venetian Senate and the Council of Ten in 1502, the first known copyright on a typeface rather than on individual titles. The privilege proved difficult to enforce but marked a milestone in the legal protection of intellectual property.

Distribution, Influence and Competition

The European Distribution Network

Aldine editions reached international markets through a sophisticated distribution infrastructure that went far beyond the popular image of the itinerant pedlar. As Lowry observes, “the actual process of distribution was by no means as primitive as is sometimes supposed” (Lowry, p. 27). Venetian printers inherited the commercial machinery of the manuscript trade: the Florentine merchant Gerolamo Strozzi, for instance, had distributed more than 1,500 volumes through agents in Rome, Siena, Pisa and Naples, and transported books to Bruges and London aboard the state-owned galleys of the Venetian Republic as early as 1476 (Lowry, pp. 27-28). By the same period, Nicolas Jenson had 500 ducats worth of stock in Pavia alone, maintained agents in most Italian centres, and employed an English contact, William Tose, to manage his affairs north of the Alps (Lowry, p. 27).

The development of international book-fairs at Frankfurt and Lyons provided printers with detailed information about production in different centres and allowed business methods to become more refined (Lowry, p. 25). These fairs were the primary wholesale clearing-houses for the European book trade, where publishers and booksellers exchanged stock, settled accounts, and negotiated rights. Aldus participated in this system, and his agent network extended through the German commercial presence in Venice. The Fondaco dei Tedeschi, the German merchants’ warehouse on the Grand Canal, served as a natural hub: Willibald Pirckheimer of Nuremberg casually noted in 1501 that there were always “one or two of his Imhof relations at the Fondaco” who would act as agents (Lowry, p. 275). Pirckheimer employed a variety of semi-official intermediaries, including the young law student Anton Kress, who shuttled between Padua and Pavia and could be dispatched to scrutinise rumours in the literary world — such as the imminent appearance of Aldus’s trilingual Bible (Lowry, p. 275). Later he relied on John Cuno and even Albrecht Durer, who was then in Venice examining the style of the Venetian painters, to investigate the bookshops on his behalf (Lowry, pp. 275-276).

The Octavo Revolution and Wider Diffusion

The octavo format — roughly 15 cm tall — proved to be the single most important factor in Aldus’s commercial success and the geographic spread of his editions. From April 1501, Aldus began issuing compact, plain-text editions of the Latin poets printed in the new italic type, priced at around 1.5 lire mocenighe per copy (Lowry, p. 107). The Venetian nobleman Johannes Thurz wrote approvingly in 1502: “I read them as I walk around on the business of the court” (Lowry, p. 42 caption). Aldus claimed to be producing 1,000 copies per month in 1503, each month’s work costing around 1,500 lire or 234 ducats (Lowry, p. 107). Lowry notes that the octavo was not an invention of the portable book — portable manuscripts had existed for centuries — but Aldus applied the format systematically to a mass market, using it for “safe and popular titles rather than treating it as a vehicle for the spread of new knowledge” (Lowry, p. 151). The octavo format was rapidly imitated across Europe, proving the most commercially successful of all Aldus’s innovations and making Aldine texts the most widely disseminated classical editions of the age.

England

Aldus’s influence on English humanism was profound, driven by a tight-knit group of English scholars who had studied in Italy. Thomas Linacre, who studied under Poliziano and Ermolao Barbaro in Florence, returned to England around 1498 bearing a magnificent vellum set of the Aldine Aristotle — one of the most expensive editions of the fifteenth century, priced at eleven ducats even on paper — which he gave to New College, Oxford; a second set was acquired by William Grocyn for Corpus Christi College (Lowry, pp. 261-262). The library of Corpus Christi, founded in 1516 by Bishop Richard Fox as the first institution in England dedicated to trilingual studies (Latin, Greek, Hebrew), contained twenty-four Aldine volumes out of thirty-three Greek items listed (Lowry, p. 262). Thomas More’s Utopia (1516) pays a subtle and sustained tribute to Aldus: the Greek texts that More’s hero Raphael gives to the Utopians — Plato, Aristotle, Theophrastus, Plutarch, Lucian, Hesychius, Homer, Aristophanes, Euripides, Sophocles, Herodotus, and Thucydides — are all Aldine editions (Lowry, p. 262). More’s protégé John Clement, one of the first scholars of St Paul’s School, owned many books “of Aldus’ prynt” (Lowry, p. 262). The relationship was one of genuine partnership: Aldus sent to England for manuscripts of Bruni’s translation of Aristotle’s Ethics and for Prudentius, and saluted Linacre as a valued collaborator from whom he hoped to receive “other most useful works in medicine and philosophy” (Lowry, pp. 262-263).

Germany and the Empire

Aldine texts were widely available in Germany from an early date, with dealers like Jordan von Dinslaken active at the Frankfurt fairs and booksellers like Johan Amerbach of Basel handling Venetian editions years before Aldus began printing (Lowry, p. 264). The first concentrated reception centred on Heidelberg, where from the mid-1490s the “Societas litteraria Rhenana” — led by Bishop Johan von Dalberg, the theologian Jodocus Gallus, the Hellenist Johann Reuchlin, and the poet Conrad Celtis — eagerly sought Greek texts and grammars from Aldus (Lowry, pp. 264-265). Aldus’s reply to Celtis in October 1497 arranged for texts to be supplied through his agent and enclosed copies of Fra Urbano’s grammar as gifts (Lowry, p. 265). The enthusiasm of the Heidelberg circle soon spread to Vienna, where Celtis founded a “Societas Danubiana” and pressed for the appointment of a professor of Greek, while the bookseller Leonard Alantsee acted as intermediary between Aldus and the Viennese scholars (Lowry, pp. 266-268). Johann Cuspinianus (Spiesshammer) provided editorial collaboration, sending Aldus twenty-four missing sections of Valerius Maximus, which the printer used to re-issue his text in 1503 with a dedication saluting Spiesshammer as a true “benefactor of the Republic of letters” (Lowry, pp. 267-268).

The most consequential German diffusion came through John Cuno, a Dominican who studied with Aldus in Venice from 1504. On his arrival in Basel toward the end of 1510, Cuno brought the ethos of the Aldine circle directly into the heart of Northern European humanism. His teaching — which relied on the spirit rather than the letter of Aldus’s example — drew Beatus Rhenanus back from Paris and provoked urgent requests from Reuchlin to persuade him to stay (Lowry, p. 269). Cuno’s pupils included the three Amerbach sons, Johan Froben, and the devoted Beatus Rhenanus, and his influence transformed the Basel print-shop into a research-centre closely modelled on Aldus’s own workshop (Lowry, pp. 270-271). Boniface Amerbach became an obsessive collector of Aldine editions; his personal copy of the Aldine Book of Hours acquired the qualities of a family Bible, in whose pages he recorded the most important events of his life (Lowry, p. 271).

The reach extended to the Elector of Saxony’s university at Wittenberg, where the humanist Mutian recommended Aldus’s Grammar to his students and lamented in 1510 that “liberal studies were lying dead, bereaved of Aldus’ help” when the Alpine passes were closed by war (Lowry, p. 277). Mutian’s pupil Georg Spalatinus, secretary to Elector Frederick the Wise, was instructed to write to Aldus for catalogues to build the university library; Wolf Fries, acting as agent, bought 153 volumes of which a specially listed section of twenty were “Aldus Druch” — between ten and fifteen percent of the total, “valued above the rest” (Lowry, pp. 277-278). As Mutian succinctly observed, the Elector of Saxony “had bought his Greek library in Venice and opened it in Wittenberg” — laying an Italian humanist foundation beneath the German Reformation (Lowry, p. 278).

France

Aldus’s penetration of the French market followed a different pattern from his English and German successes. Though many Frenchmen came to Italy to study, French literary traditions were more deeply entrenched, and much of the initiative for scholarly contact came from Aldus himself (Lowry, p. 279). He cultivated the French administrative presence in Milan, dedicating editions to the secretary Antiquario and the diplomat Jeffroy Charles, and established links with the French ambassador in Venice, Accursius de Mayner (Lowry, pp. 279-280). But the most important conduit was Jean Grolier, the French royal treasurer in Milan, who became perhaps the most devoted collector of Aldines in Europe. Of roughly 350 surviving volumes from Grolier’s library, almost half are Aldines — forty-two of them from the lifetime of Aldus the Elder. Grolier owned four copies of the 1501 Juvenal, four of the Hypnerotomachia Polifili, and six of the first octavo Martial, often on vellum with specially commissioned illumination (Lowry, pp. 280-281). In 1515 Musurus dedicated the posthumous edition of Aldus’s Greek Grammar to Grolier, asking for his help in persuading Andrea Torresani to maintain Aldus’s standards (Lowry, p. 281).

Meanwhile, Aldus’s Greek editions were fueling the emergence of Hellenic studies in Paris. Francois Tissard returned from Italy in 1507 and funded Gilles de Gourmont to print the first Greek texts in France, openly copying the Aldine edition of 1495 (Lowry, p. 283). Gerolamo Aleander, who had worked on the texts of Plutarch’s Moralia and Erasmus’s Adagia in Aldus’s workshop, arrived in Paris in June 1508 bringing three cases of Aldine books. His lectures packed the halls — courtiers came to hear him, students arrived two hours early — and he was elected Rector of the University in 1513, the first Italian to hold the post in two centuries. None of this would have been possible without the foundation of Aldus’s Greek editions (Lowry, pp. 283-284). The French Royal Library under Francis I, assembled by Guillaume Pellicier, contained fifty-five printed texts of which thirty-one were Aldines, sixteen from the lifetime of the elder Aldus; no more than two editions came from any other printing-house (Lowry, pp. 285-286).

Influence on Later Printers: Froben and the Estiennes

The Aldine influence on Northern European printing was most visible in Basel and Paris. Johan Froben, who took over the Amerbach press in 1514, began with Gothic founts but gradually drifted toward Italian styles. During the first and second decades of the new century he used Amerbach’s bold Roman types and a Greek fount bearing obvious resemblances to the Aldine cursive. In 1519 he introduced two close copies of the Aldine italic — the first legitimate ventures into this style by a Northern publisher, soon widely imitated (Lowry, pp. 273-274). Froben also re-issued a number of Aldine editions, beginning with Erasmus’s Adagia in 1513, followed by an octavo Aesop, Theodorus Gaza’s Greek Grammar, and Erasmus’s translations from Euripides. Erasmus himself commented that Froben was “carrying on the work of Aldus” — more than a vapid compliment to both parties (Lowry, p. 273).

In France, the adoption of Aldine typography came more slowly but proved equally lasting. Simon de Colines produced a Catullus in italic in 1529, and was gradually followed by others, notably Guillaume Gryphius of Lyons with his “Great Italic” in 1537 and Robert Estienne, who interspersed his texts with italic passages until, with the De Re Rustica of 1543, he initiated a complete series of classical texts in italic (Lowry, p. 284). The Aldine Roman types — in particular the fount used for Pietro Bembo’s De Aetna (1495) — were studied and imitated through the agency of Geofroy Tory and the typefounder Claude Garamond. By the early 1530s, Colines and Estienne were using Roman founts modelled on the Aldine archetype, and from Garamond’s workshop this style spread rapidly across Europe. When Garamond took to printing on his own account in 1545, his first aim was to “copy the italic script of Aldus Manutius” (Lowry, pp. 284-285).

Eastern Europe and Spain

Aldus’s influence extended to the furthest reaches of Europe. In Hungary, the Thurz family — Sigismund Thurz, a student of Raphael Regius in Padua, became one of the first patrons to receive sample copies of the new octavos, and his request for a similar edition of Cicero’s Letters was answered with a specially drafted dedication in 1502. By 1506 Thurz held four bishoprics, the prefecture of Transylvania, and the post of secretary to the King of Poland (Lowry, pp. 287-288). Philip Csulai, the Hungarian ambassador in Venice and later Bishop of Pecs, was another close contact. In Poland, a cadre of humanist exiles strove to plant a colony of Hellenists in the University of Cracow, and Aldine texts formed the core of their resources (Lowry, pp. 287-288). In Spain, Demetrius Ducas — one of Aldus’s Greek editors — was invited in 1513 to teach at Cardinal Ximenes’ new trilingual college at Alcala. When Ducas arrived, the university possessed only fourteen Greek texts; at least seven, and possibly ten, of them were Aldine publications (Lowry, p. 286). The famous Complutensian Polyglot Bible, printed at Alcala between 1514 and 1517, abandoned its original Gothic types for a Roman-style Latin fount and a Greek cursive following the pattern set by Aldus in the 1490s (Lowry, pp. 286-287).

Piracy and Competition

This very success attracted piracy. By 1503, Lyons had become the principal centre for counterfeit Aldine editions: Barthelemy Troth and Balthazar de Gabiano produced at least sixty-four pirated editions between 1501 and 1527, using coarse imitations of the italic type and cheap paper. Aldus’s “Admonition against the Lyons typographers” (1503) tried to educate customers to distinguish genuine from forged copies, but the counterfeiters responded by improving their imitations. The Florentine printer Filippo Giunti also produced unauthorised reprints of Aldine texts between 1503 and 1505, leading to a lawsuit resolved shortly before Aldus resumed production in late 1507.

Later History and Legacy

Aldus Manutius died in 1515, a “crushed and disillusioned man” according to Lowry, unable to see the success that posterity would accord him. The press continued under the direction of his heirs — first his son Paolo Manuzio (Paulus Manutius), then his grandson Aldo Manuzio the Younger — operating intermittently until the late 1590s. The Barbarigo and Torresani heirs liquidated the company’s remaining stocks around 1542. By the time the press ceased in 1597, it had set the standard for scholarly publishing and typographical excellence across Europe. The Aldine device — a dolphin wrapped around an anchor — became the most famous printer’s mark in history, and the octavo format and italic type that Aldus pioneered remain central to book design to the present day.
