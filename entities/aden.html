Aden

Aden at the mouth of the Red Sea — the transshipment point where Indian Ocean spices were reloaded into smaller vessels for the passage north to Jiddah. The spice trade map shows the red sea route (orange) beginning at Aden before crossing to the Persian Gulf branch (teal).

Aden — Arabic: عدن (‘Adan), meaning “settled place” or “paradise” in the old South Arabian tradition — was the maritime gateway to the Red Sea and the most strategically positioned port on the spice route between India and the Mediterranean world. Sitting on a volcanic peninsula at the southwestern tip of Arabia, it commanded the narrow Bab el-Mandeb strait (“Gate of Tears”, Arabic: باب المندب) through which virtually all Indian Ocean maritime traffic had to pass to enter the Red Sea. For the Venetian spice trade, Aden was a chokepoint that Venetian merchants did not themselves operate but whose political condition — whether its ruler was rapacious or cooperative, whether it was under Mamluk authority or autonomous — directly determined the price of pepper at the Rialto.

The Transshipment Function

The Red Sea could not be navigated by the large ocean-going dhows (daw, Arabic: داو) and junks that carried pepper, cinnamon, and ginger from Indian ports such as Calicut (Kozhikode). Shallow waters, coral reefs, and the capricious reversing monsoon — the kharif (summer southwest) and shamal (winter northeast) winds — required smaller, more maneuverable vessels for the northward passage. Aden thus served as the natural entrepôt where Indian, Arab, and African maritime commerce converged: cargoes were broken down, warehoused, and reloaded for the final leg north to Jiddah, whence spices crossed the desert to the Nile and descended to Alexandria (Lane, pp. 14831–14833).

The port levied substantial transit dues — the maks (Arabic: مكس, toll or customs duty) — on every cargo passing through. The ruler of Aden, a series of Muslim sultans of the Rasulid and later Tahirid dynasties, depended on these revenues as their primary income. This made the port a consistent extractor of surplus from the India trade and created permanent incentive for Indian merchants to seek alternative routes whenever conditions became intolerable.

The Aden-Jiddah Rivalry

In the 1420s, a critical rivalry developed between Aden and Jiddah as the preferred transshipment point. The Indian sea captain Ibrahim, seeking an alternative to Aden’s exactions — the ruler “was particularly rapacious in the 1420’s” — tried Jiddah in 1422 but was pillaged by the local sharif who controlled both Jiddah and Mecca. He tried again in 1424 and received favorable treatment from a newly arrived Mamluk governor whom the Egyptian sultan had sent with soldiers accompanying the annual hajj (pilgrimage) caravan. These Mamluks brought Mecca and Jiddah directly under the sultan’s control. “The extortionate ruler of Aden found his port deserted and even Chinese vessels went directly to Jiddah” (Lane, pp. 14831–14844).

The Mamluk takeover was decisive for Venetian interests. The elimination of the high levies at both Aden and Jiddah through concentration of control in Egyptian hands “reduced the costs of protection on the Red Sea route and confirmed its superiority over any other known route between Europe and the Indies.” With the Mamluk sultan providing reliable policing and predictable tariffs, the Red Sea funnel became the most efficient path for Indian goods reaching the Mediterranean — and Venice, as the dominant buyer at Alexandria, was the primary beneficiary.

Aden’s Physical Geography and Monsoon Dynamics

The spice trade through Aden was governed by the Indian Ocean monsoon system. From November to March, the northeast monsoon (Arabic: rih al-shamal, “north wind”) drove ships from India westward toward Aden and the Arabian coast. From June to September, the southwest monsoon (rih al-kharif) reversed, permitting the return voyage to India. This meant that the flow of spices into Aden was seasonal, concentrated in winter arrivals. Merchants and factors had to time their operations carefully: spices purchased at Aden in January or February could be forwarded to Jiddah and reach the Alexandrian market in time for the summer sailings of the Venetian galley muda.

The narrowness of the Bab el-Mandeb strait — barely 30 kilometres at its minimum width — also concentrated all maritime traffic at Aden. Any power that held the port could in theory tax or interdict the entire India trade, a fact that the Portuguese would later exploit in their attempts to blockade the strait.

Venetian Interest and Indirect Involvement

Venetians rarely sailed to Aden themselves — their commercial presence was concentrated at Alexandria, Beirut, and Damascus, the Mediterranean endpoints of the spice funnel. However, the Republic maintained informal intelligence about conditions throughout the route, and Venetian merchants were well aware of political developments at Aden. When the ruler’s exactions drove merchants to Jiddah, the shift benefited Venetian buyers by consolidating the spice funnel under Mamluk control, reducing the cumulative tolls that had inflated prices along the route.

The Venetian Senate tracked conditions at Aden with the care one would expect of a government whose fiscal health depended on the spice trade. Ambassadors to the Mamluk sultan at Cairo included in their dispatches information about the Red Sea ports, and the bailo (commercial representative and ambassador) at Alexandria maintained contacts with Mamluk officials who controlled the route.

The Portuguese Challenge

After Vasco da Gama’s first voyage to India (1498) and the subsequent Portuguese establishment in the Indian Ocean, Aden became a flashpoint of imperial competition. Portuguese fleets under Afonso de Albuquerque attempted to seize Aden in 1513, recognising that control of the port would strangle the Red Sea spice route and give Portugal a monopoly over the India trade. The assault failed — Aden’s volcanic basalt walls proved impregnable from the sea — and the Portuguese “never succeeded in taking Aden or stopping entirely the flow of spices” (Lane, pp. 15076–15077).

This failure was crucial. Unlike Ormuz, which the Portuguese did capture in 1507, Aden remained in local and later Ottoman hands throughout the sixteenth century. Portuguese patrols at the Bab el-Mandeb disrupted the trade but could not shut it down entirely; leakage through Aden continued to supply the Red Sea route with Indian spices, and Venice continued to buy at Alexandria, though in diminished quantities.

Ottoman Conquest and the Route’s Revival

The Ottoman conquest of Egypt in 1517 placed Aden under the suzerainty of a vastly more formidable power than the Mamluks. By 1538 the Ottomans had extended their direct control to Aden itself, installing a governor and a garrison. This brought the Portuguese in the Indian Ocean face to face with Ottoman naval power. The Ottomans “controlled Aden,” making Portuguese efforts to patrol the straits increasingly ineffective (Lane, p. 15150).

Under Ottoman rule, Aden became the base for an ambitious but ultimately unsuccessful campaign to drive the Portuguese from the Indian Ocean. Suleiman the Magnificent despatched fleets through the Red Sea in the 1530s and 1540s; these operations, though they failed to dislodge the Portuguese from their Indian bases, demonstrated Ottoman commitment to the Red Sea route. By the 1560s, the flow of spices through Aden and the Red Sea had revived to levels comparable to the 1490s, contributing to the commercial renaissance that sustained Venice’s Levantine trade into the later sixteenth century.
