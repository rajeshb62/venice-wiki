Gattamelata (Erasmo da Narni)

Erasmo da Narni (c. 1370–1443), better known as Gattamelata — “the Honeyed Cat” (gatta, cat; mellata, honeyed, sweet) — was the most celebrated Venetian condottiero of the early 15th century. He is immortalised by Donatello’s bronze equestrian statue in Padua — the first monumental equestrian bronze cast since antiquity and a landmark of Renaissance sculpture.

The Condottiere System

Venice’s military system in the fifteenth century rested on the condotta (Italian: contratto di guerra, war contract) — an agreement between the Republic and a hired military captain who raised, equipped, and commanded a private army for a fixed period and fee. The Republic maintained almost no permanent standing army of citizen soldiers; instead, it hired professional captains (condottieri, from condotta) who maintained retinues of mounted heavy cavalry (uomini d’arme, men-at-arms) and lighter cavalry (stradioti, cavalry of Albanian or Greek origin), as well as infantry.

This system gave Venice military power without requiring it to arm its own citizens — a deliberate choice by a ruling oligarchy deeply suspicious of any concentration of arms outside the state’s control. The condottieri were paid in cash, rewarded with gifts of land and titles, and celebrated in public art — but they were never permitted to accumulate power within Venice itself. Their monuments, like Gattamelata’s statue, stood in subject cities like Padua, not in the Serenissima.

Career and Venetian Service

Gattamelata was born in Narni, a hilltop town in Umbria, and began his military career serving various Italian signori — including the Papacy and the Florentine Republic — before entering Venetian employment in the 1430s. His sobriquet “Gattamelata” was allegedly given to him because of his cunning, smooth-tongued diplomatic manner in negotiations — he could be conciliatory and sweet-spoken (mellato) while pursuing his objectives with feline patience.

During the wars between Venice and Milan under Doge Francesco Foscari — the great struggle for the Lombard plain that consumed the Republic from 1423 to the 1450s — Gattamelata became Venice’s leading general in the Lombardy campaigns. Lane records that he “commanded Venice’s efforts to relieve Brescia, and some historians give him credit for the hauling of ships over the mountains to the Lago di Garda” (Lane, p. 233). This last feat — dragging a fleet of small vessels overland across the Alpine foothills to launch a surprise attack on the fortified lake — was a preview of the daring logistical operations that Venetian commanders would occasionally attempt. The Lago di Garda operation, if accurately attributed, involved disassembling galleys and brigantini (small galleys) at Mori in the Adige valley and transporting them on ox-sledges over a mountain pass to the lake, then reassembling and launching them to outmanoeuvre the Visconti forces defending Brescia. The relief of Brescia (1438) was one of the most dramatic episodes of the Lombard wars.

Loyalty and Reputation

Gattamelata served Venice with consistent loyalty throughout his career. Lane characterises him as a commander who “served without brilliant successes but with constant loyalty” (Lane, p. 233). This was an important quality in an age when condottieri routinely changed sides — Carmagnola had betrayed Milan for Venice, and Venice would later execute him for betraying Venice back to Milan. Gattamelata’s reliability made him a trusted instrument of Venetian policy.

His relationship with the Republic was genuinely reciprocal. Venice honoured Gattamelata in his lifetime with gifts, pensions, and political privileges, and after his death in Padua in 1443 it commissioned the monument that would make him immortal. This was not standard treatment: most condottieri received their pay and left no permanent mark on Venetian public space. Gattamelata was exceptional.

Donatello’s Equestrian Statue

Gattamelata’s enduring fame rests on Donatello’s bronze equestrian statue, erected in the Piazza del Santo in front of the Basilica di Sant’Antonio (il Santo) in Padua. Donatello worked on the statue in Padua between 1443 and 1453, during his decade-long residence in the city — the same period in which he deeply influenced the young Andrea Mantegna and transformed Paduan art. Lane calls it “the first equestrian statue worthy of comparison with those of antiquity, recalling indeed the statue of Marcus Aurelius in Rome” (Lane, pp. 231, 1214).

The statue shows Gattamelata in full Roman-influenced armour (armatura all’antica) astride a powerful warhorse, his bastone di comando (baton of command) held at rest. The horse’s left foreleg rests on a cannonball — a symbol of the new age of gunpowder warfare that was transforming the battlefield Gattamelata had commanded. The work was revolutionary in its naturalism, its classical inspiration, and its technical ambition. Cast in bronze using the lost-wax (cera persa) method, it stood over 3.4 metres high and required Donatello to solve immense engineering problems of casting, transportation, and mounting. The commission may have been obtained partly through the lobbying of Gattamelata’s family and the Venetian state, and it was paid for with money from Gattamelata’s estate — not purely from public funds, as legend sometimes suggests.

Donatello had come to Padua partly because “Florentine influence was directly felt in Padua through the presence there of Donatello and of the leading patron of Florentine humanists, Palla Strozzi, exiled by his rival Cosimo de’ Medici” (Lane, p. 1209). After ten years in Padua, Donatello returned to Florence, saying “only in Florence could he receive the stimulus of constant criticism” (Lane, p. 1211). The Gattamelata statue was his most important work from this Paduan period and the one that most directly influenced the future of European sculpture.

Significance in Venetian History

The Gattamelata monument marked a turning point in both Renaissance art and the social status of condottieri. For the first time since Roman antiquity, a secular military commander was honoured with a public bronze equestrian monument — a form previously reserved for emperors and saints. The location — Padua, a subject city under Venetian rule, not Venice itself — was deliberate. Venice’s oligarchic government was notoriously suspicious of individual glorification: no Venetian noble was permitted to erect a personal monument in the city. By placing the statue in Padua, the Republic could honour its servant without creating a precedent for public hero worship within the Serenissima itself.

The statue established a visual type that would be imitated throughout Europe, most immediately in Venice itself where Verrocchio’s equestrian monument to Bartolomeo Colleoni (another Venetian condottiero) was erected in the Campo dei Santi Giovanni e Paolo between 1488 and 1496. The Colleoni monument was deliberately placed not in the Piazza San Marco — Colleoni had bequeathed money for an equestrian statue “before San Marco,” and the Senate satisfied the letter of this bequest by placing it before the Scuola Grande di San Marco, not the Basilica — another example of Venetian ingenuity in avoiding the glorification of individuals in the Republic’s sacred centre.
