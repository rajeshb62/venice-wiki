Padua (Padova) was the first major mainland city acquired by Venice (1405) and became the second city of the Venetian terraferma state. Its university, printing industry, and artistic workshops made it the intellectual and cultural powerhouse of the Republic.

Mainland Acquisition

Padua submitted to Venice in 1405 during the expansion under Doge Tommaso Mocenigo (Lane, timeline). This was Venice’s first major terra firma acquisition and it set the pattern for the mainland empire. Padua’s fertile contado supplied grain to the lagoon city, and its position on the Brenta river gave Venice a strategic gateway into the Veneto interior. The relationship was never entirely comfortable: Paduans retained a strong civic identity and occasional resentment of Venetian rule (Lane, p. 12889 notes a Venetian diarist who “denounced Paduans with special vigor”).

The University of Padua

Founded in 1222 by a secession from Bologna, the University of Padua (the Studium) became “Venice’s own” after the conquest (Lane, p. 236). The Venetian government forbade its nobles to study elsewhere, funded distinguished professors, and elected a board of Riformatori dello Studio di Padova to govern the institution (Lane, pp. 236-237). Law was the largest faculty and the one most attended by Venetian nobles preparing for government service. The medical faculty was equally distinguished; no competing medical schools within Venetian domains were permitted to grant degrees, and the Paduan medical degree became necessary for full status in the Venetian medical guild (Lane, pp. 236-237).

In 1463 Venice established a chair for Greek at Padua, making the city a centre of humanistic studies (Lane, p. 239). The humanist educator Guarino Guarini taught in Venice and influenced the Paduan intellectual milieu; his pupil Francesco Barbaro became the model of Venetian civic humanism (Lane, pp. 239-240).

Printing and the Aldine Connection

Padua’s university created a ready market for printed books, and the Venetian printing industry was “perfectly placed to supply the nearby universities of Padua” (Lowry, p. 35). Cooperation between Padua and Venetian printers was very close — the university town was barely twenty miles from Venice, ruled by the same Republic, and much frequented by its citizens (Lowry, p. 35). Academic lawyers from Padua edited and corrected texts for Venetian presses. The Aldine Press was dependent on the scribes of Venice and Padua for copy, and Aldus modelled both his types and his printed pages on their manuscripts (Angerhofer, p. 66). Pietro Bembo retired to Padua in 1521 for a period of intense intellectual activity, revising Gli Asolani there (Angerhofer, p. 7).

Crisis of 1509: Recapture and Siege

After the Venetian defeat at Agnadello (1509), Padua fell to the League of Cambrai without resistance. It became the critical target of Andrea Gritti’s counter-attack. On the night of 16 July 1509, barges crowded with armed volunteers filled the canals of Venice; the next day Gritti’s force retook Padua, “welcomed by partisans within the walls shouting ‘Marco, Marco’” (Lane, p. 245). Emperor Maximilian then besieged the city with “the most formidable artillery yet used in any siege” (Lane, p. 245). The successful defence under Gritti’s command was the turning point of the Cambrai war, saving Venice’s mainland empire.

Art and the Paduan School

Padua was the artistic gateway through which Florentine Renaissance influence entered Venetian painting (Lane, p. 230). In the 1440s, Donatello worked in Padua for a decade, leaving behind the bronze equestrian statue of Gattamelata — the first monumental equestrian bronze since antiquity (Lane, p. 231). Andrea Mantegna, formed in Padua under Squarcione and Donatello, painted the Eremitani frescoes (destroyed in World War II) and carried the Paduan style northward (Lane, p. 232). Berenson notes that Giovanni Bellini was “formed in Padua under the influence of Donatello” (Berenson, p. 3401), and Gentile Bellini was “influenced by the Paduans” (Berenson, p. 3375). The Paduan bottega of Francesco Squarcione, who claimed 137 apprentices, was the largest painting workshop of its time (Lane, p. 230).

Banking and Finance

Padua had its own banking sector, and Paduan bankers were active in the Venetian money market. Mueller records the case of Petrus de Mortisse, “an immigrant from Padua and naturalized Venetian” who operated as a campsor (money-changer) at San Marco in the 14th century and became entangled in a dispute over real estate in Padua (Mueller, pp. 3077-3095). The fiscal relationship between Venice and the contado padovano was a subject of continuous negotiation, with tax surveys and assessments recorded in Paduan archives (Mueller, citing Knapton).

Relationship with Venice

Padua was the second city of the Venetian mainland state — large, prosperous, intellectually dominant, but politically subordinate. Venetian nobles studied there, Venetian governors ruled there, and Venetian printers supplied its university. Yet Padua retained its own civic traditions, its powerful guilds of lawyers and notaries, and its distinctive artistic and intellectual identity. The French have sometimes called Padua Venice’s “Left Bank” — a phrase Lane employs to capture the intimate cultural union between the lagoon capital and its mainland counterpart (Lane, p. 230).
