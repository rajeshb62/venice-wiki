Francesco Griffo (Francesco da Bologna)

Francesco Griffo (also known as Francesco da Bologna) was the most accomplished punchcutter and type designer of the early Aldine Press, active in Venice from the early 1490s until about 1502. He designed and cut virtually all of Aldus Manutius’s type-founts — including the first italic type (1501), the roman used for the Hypnerotomachia Polifili (1499), and multiple Greek cursive founts. His work defined the typographic aesthetic of the Aldine Press and, through the italic in particular, influenced book design across Europe for centuries.

Life

Very little is known of Griffo’s early life. He was from Bologna — hence his alternative name “Francesco da Bologna” — and was almost certainly a trained goldsmith or metalworker before turning to punchcutting. Lowry notes that there are “only three fixed points from which we can investigate Francesco’s connection with Venetian printing” (p. 108):

  1. The three-line tribute (terzain) added by Aldus to the first text printed in italic cursive, the Virgil of 1501.
  2. The claim made two years later by Gerolamo Soncino of Fano, that he had secured the services of the expert who had cut all Aldus’s types.
  3. The repetition of that claim by Francesco himself, in an edition of Petrarch’s Canzonieri which he published independently in 1516.

His connection with Aldus lasted ten or twelve years at most, and for only seven of these was the Aldine publishing company in active production (p. 109).

Death

After leaving Aldus, Griffo attempted independent work as a publisher-printer. The strains of independent action appear to have been too great. Lowry records that “shortly afterwards he brained his son-in-law with an iron bar — perhaps an incomplete type punch — faced a murder-charge in Bologna with unknown results, and sank into an obscurity from which the most searching efforts of scholarship have still failed to rouse him” (p. 108). According to Mardersteig (cited in Lowry’s note 70), the attack on his son-in-law occurred in May 1518, and by October 1519 Francesco was referred to as deceased.

Work for Aldus Manutius

The Roman Type for Hypnerotomachia Polifili (1499)

Griffo cut the roman type used for the celebrated Hypnerotomachia Polifili, the elaborate allegorical romance printed in December 1499. This was Aldus’s most visually ambitious publication, and the type — a refined, open roman — contributed significantly to the book’s reputation as one of the most beautiful printed books of the Renaissance. The same type was also used for other Aldine productions of the period (Lowry, p. 133, note on type of Polifilo).

The First Italic Type (1501)

Griffo’s most famous creation is the Aldine italic, first used for the Virgil of April 1501. The type was based on the cursive chancery hand (cancelleresca) then fashionable among humanist scribes, particularly the style associated with Niccolo Niccoli and later practitioners such as Bartolomeo di San Vito and Antonio de’ Tagliente. Aldus introduced the type with a three-line tribute in Latin elegiac couplets (a terzain) that credits Griffo by name:

Qui Gratis dedit Aldus, en Latinis Dat nunc grammata scalpta daedaleis Francisci manibus Bononiensis.

(“Aldus, who gave you Latin texts for nothing, now gives you letters carved by the Daedalean hands of Francesco of Bologna.“)

The italic was not designed — as is often mistakenly assumed — to economise on space by cramming more letters onto each page. Lowry argues convincingly that Aldus’s aim was aesthetic: to give his printed texts the same status and elegance as the most fashionable manuscript books, which had long used the cursive hand. The italic was a continuation of the humanistic Roman style, not a departure from it (pp. 160-161).

Greek Types

Griffo cut four Greek cursive founts for Aldus, each similar in basic conception but differing in detail. These were used for the landmark series of Greek editions — Aristotle, Aristophanes, Sophocles, the Greek dramatists — that formed the core of Aldus’s programme. One of the technical innovations attributed to Griffo is a system of “kerning” accents into the top of vowel sorts, avoiding the awkward overprinting of separate accent pieces (p. 110).

Hebrew Type

Griffo also cut a Hebrew fount for Aldus. Though never applied to the ambitious trilingual Bible projects Aldus planned, it was used sporadically throughout his lifetime (p. 109).

Twelve Type-Founts in Three Languages

Altogether, during his association with Aldus, Griffo cut twelve type-founts: six rounded (Roman) Latin types, the famous italic, four Greek cursives, and one Hebrew fount. Lowry writes: “When we speak of Aldine typography, we are speaking of Griffo’s workmanship” (p. 109). After Griffo’s departure in 1502, no new type designs were introduced by the Aldine Press.

The Dispute over Credit

The relationship between Aldus and Griffo ended in acrimony. Griffo’s departure from Venice late in 1502 coincides closely with Aldus’s grant of a comprehensive monopoly (14 November 1502) over all Greek publication and all Latin printing in italic within the Venetian dominions.

Gerolamo Soncino explicitly charged that Aldus and others had usurped the credit for inventions made by Griffo, whom he described as “a most excellent cutter of Latin, Greek and Hebrew letters, a man whose genius finds no equal in this craft” (p. 108). The contradiction between Aldus’s claims in his petitions for privilege — that he had “discovered two new methods” or “devised a new kind of letter called cursive” — and the counterclaims of Soncino and Griffo himself could not be resolved in the legal framework of the time, which protected only the investor, not the craftsman (pp. 109-111).

Lowry suggests that the breach was less a matter of personal clash than of changing commercial structures. Aldus was moving away from hands-on technical involvement toward organisation and public relations, while Griffo belonged to the older world of the master-craftsman who directed every stage of production. “When men of such wildly different background and commitment as these last two were doing the same job, and when the concept of a specialised service-industry had yet to emerge, it was almost inevitable that a man like Griffo would get hurt” (p. 111).

Work after Aldus: Gerson Soncino

After leaving Aldus, Griffo worked for Gerolamo (Gerson) Soncino, a Jewish printer active in Fano. If Griffo designed and cut the punches for Aldus’s last distinct type (the small Greek cursive for Sophocles, August 1502), then equipped Soncino with a Latin italic fount in time for an edition by 7 July 1503, the signs are that he left Venice late in 1502 (p. 109).

Independent Printing

In 1516, Griffo published his own edition of Petrarch’s Canzonieri, in which he repeated the claim that he, not Aldus, deserved the credit for the Aldine types. This venture appears to have been a financial and personal failure. Soon afterward came the murder of his son-in-law and his disappearance from the historical record.

Legacy

Despite his obscurity — no portrait, no surviving correspondence, only a handful of documentary references — Francesco Griffo’s influence on Western typography is incalculable:

  • The Aldine italic, cut in 1501, became the model for virtually all italic types that followed. It was imitated within years by printers in Lyon, Florence, and elsewhere, and its basic form remains in use today.
  • The roman type of the Hypnerotomachia Polifili is regarded as one of the finest of the incunable period.
  • His Greek types set the standard for printing classical texts for decades, and the technical innovation of kerning accents shows his practical ingenuity.

Lowry’s judgment is definitive: “Experiment ended with Griffo’s departure” from the Aldine Press (p. 109).
