Pietro Bembo

Pietro Bembo (1470–1547) was the most celebrated Venetian man of letters of the High Renaissance — a poet, scholar, literary theorist, historian, and cardinal who shaped the Italian literary language and served as the official historian of Venice. His life illustrates the tension between the Venetian ideal of political service and the Renaissance ideal of literary and scholarly retirement.

Early Life and Education

Bembo was born into one of Venice’s most distinguished noble families. His father, Bernardo Bembo, was a prominent diplomat and humanist who ensured that Pietro received the finest humanist education available. The young Bembo studied Greek under Constantine Lascaris in Messina, then philosophy and literature in Florence and Padua. He was trained for public service — his father took him on embassies to foreign courts — but Pietro’s inclinations were literary rather than political (Lane, pp. 240–241).

Life Away from Venice

Pietro spent much of his early adult life away from Venice, a fact that Lane attributes to his temperament: he “preferred love-making and poetry to negotiations and administration” (p. 240). He lived in Ferrara (where he conducted a celebrated love affair with Lucrezia Borgia), Urbino (where he took part in the conversations recorded in Castiglione’s The Book of the Courtier), and Rome (where he served as secretary to Pope Leo X).

During these years he produced his most important literary works:

  • Gli Asolani (1505): a dialogue on Platonic love, set in the gardens of the court of Asolo, where the exiled Queen Caterina Cornaro of Cyprus held court
  • Prose della volgar lingua (1525): the foundational text of Italian literary linguistics. Bembo argued that the model for Italian literary prose should be the language of Petrarch and Boccaccio — 14th-century Tuscan — rather than Latin or contemporary vernaculars. This work effectively standardised Italian literary language for the next three centuries.
  • Rime: Petrarchan love poems that established him as the leading poet of his generation

Aldine Collaboration

Bembo’s career was intimately tied to the Aldine Press and to its founder, Aldus Manutius. Their association began almost as soon as Aldus established his print-shop in Venice in the 1490s and lasted for the remainder of Aldus’ life, encompassing editorial collaboration, scholarly exchange, and a shared vision for the revival of classical learning.

Early Association with Aldus

Bembo first appears in Aldus’ orbit as one of the young noblemen who studied under Giorgio Valla, the public lecturer through whose connections Aldus entered Venetian high society (Lowry, pp. 192, 211). Together with Angelo Gabriel, Bembo was sent to Messina to study Greek under Constantine Lascaris. They returned in late 1494 bringing back a manuscript of Lascaris’ Greek Grammar corrected by the author’s own hand in “around one hundred and fifty places” — this manuscript became the basis for Aldus’ first dated publication, the Erotemata of 1495 (Lowry, pp. 224-225).

Bembo’s own first publication with Aldus followed closely: De Aetna (February 1496), a short Latin essay describing his ascent of Mount Etna during the Sicilian journey. The piece was a personal favour between friends, but its typographical significance was immense — the Roman type cut by Francesco Griffo for this slim volume has become so famous that it is still identified by the name of that “otherwise insignificant piece.” Stanley Morison called the De Aetna fount the opening of “a new epoch in typography” (Lowry, pp. 136, 155-156).

The Aldine Academy (Neacademia)

Bembo was a key figure in Aldus’ New Academy (Neacademia or Aldi Academia), the informal learned society whose statute was drafted around 1502. The Academy’s members bound themselves to speak only Greek in one another’s company, and its activities included philological discussion, lectures, and the preparation of texts for the Aldine press. Though the statute itself mentions only seven names — Aldus as “leader,” Fortiguerra, “John the Cretan,” Egnazio, Paolo da Canal, Menochio, and Rosetto — the circle extended far wider and Bembo was among its most significant participants (Lowry, pp. 196-198).

Lowry notes a tension within the Academy between dedicated Hellenists who regarded Greek as all-important, and figures like Bembo who were equally interested in contemporary Latin literature and the directions the Italian vernacular might take (Lowry, p. 199). This tension reflected Bembo’s own dual commitment to classical erudition and to the development of a standardised Italian literary language — the project that would culminate in his Prose della volgar lingua (1525).

Editorial Work: Petrarch and Dante

Bembo’s most celebrated contribution to the Aldine press was his editorial work on the great Italian vernacular poets. In 1501 Aldus published Le cose volgari di Messer Francesco Petrarca, a landmark edition of Petrarch’s vernacular poetry based — or so Aldus and Bembo claimed — on an autograph manuscript. The edition’s colophon, lodged by Bembo’s brother Carlo, made the claim official, and Aldus composed a spirited defence when it was challenged. Modern scholarship suggests that Bembo may have used his own annotated copy (Vaticanus Latinus 3197) as the basis, introducing around 160 deviations from the original, consciously intervening in matters of spelling and even altering the meaning of certain passages (Lowry, pp. 225-227).

In 1502 Aldus published Bembo’s edition of Dante’s Terze rime (the Divine Comedy), again based on manuscripts that Bembo had assembled and collated. These two editions — Petrarch in 1501 and Dante in 1502 — were foundational texts for the emerging Italian literary canon and established the Aldine octavo format as the standard for vernacular poetry (Lowry, pp. 223-224).

Gli Asolani (1505)

Bembo’s dialogue on Platonic love, Gli Asolani, was published by Aldus in 1505. Set in the gardens of the court of Asolo, where the exiled Queen Caterina Cornaro of Cyprus held court, the work represented a popularisation of Marsilio Ficino’s theories of love and a crucial literary experiment in the vernacular. To Aldus’ more austere academic friends like John Cuno, the book seemed trivial — “a few odds and ends in the vernacular, about Love” — but for Aldus it represented a commercially sensible expansion beyond the Greek programme. The Asolani was among the Aldine titles for which the printer held standard Venetian copyrights (Lowry, pp. 152-153, 174).

The Italic Type and Bembo’s Handwriting

The Aldine italic type — first used in the Virgil of April 1501 — has long been connected to Bembo’s handwriting. Lowry identifies the Paduan scribe Bartolomeo di San Vito (Bartolomeo Sanvito) as a likely model for Francesco Griffo’s italic punches, noting that Sanvito “not only wrote a hand very similar in form to the Aldine but can be proved to have dealt frequently with a number of the printer’s closest associates, including … the Bembo family, for whom he copied at least four manuscripts” (Lowry, p. 161). Bembo’s own elegant humanist script, influenced by Sanvito’s style, has often been cited as the direct inspiration for the Aldine italic — one of the most influential type designs in Western printing history.

Bembo also gave Aldus the Roman coin bearing a dolphin and anchor which became Aldus’ famous printer’s device, and which Erasmus later discussed in his proverb “Hasten slowly” (Festina lente) (Lowry, p. 81).

Correspondence and Patronage

When Pope Leo X ascended the papal throne in 1513, he immediately appointed Bembo as his apostolic secretary. From this position Bembo was able to advance the cause of Aldus and his learned circle, being instructed to contact Marcus Musurus about the possibility of establishing a Greek College in Rome. Bembo’s influence in papal Rome kept the Aldine Academy’s dreams alive even as the political turmoil of the Italian Wars frustrated their realisation (Lowry, p. 205).

Throughout their long association, Bembo’s relationship with Aldus was one of mutual benefit: Bembo provided invaluable manuscripts and editorial expertise for Lascaris’ Grammar, Petrarch’s Cose volgari, and Dante’s Commedia, and in return Aldus published Bembo’s own works — De Aetna and Gli Asolani — launching one of the most brilliant literary careers of the Italian Renaissance (Lowry, p. 223).

In 1530, after decades of delay, the Venetian Senate appointed Bembo as the Republic’s official historian. The position had been created to continue the work of Sabellico, whose official history had been commissioned in the 1480s. Bembo was given access to the state archives and to the notes of Marin Sanuto’s diary.

Bembo’s History of Venice (published in Latin as Rerum Venetarum Historiae) covered the period from 1487 to 1513 — the years of the League of Cambrai and the Italian Wars that Bembo himself had lived through. Lane notes that Bembo “commanded a finer Latin style” than Sanuto, but his history was less useful to the Senate than Sabellico’s had been; it was admired for its literary quality rather than its political analysis (p. 241).

Later Life and Legacy

In 1539, Bembo was made a cardinal by Pope Paul III, despite having fathered three children. He spent his final years in Rome, continuing to write and to act as a patron of scholarship. He died in 1547 and was buried in the church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva.

Bembo’s legacy is paradoxical. He was a Venetian noble who spent most of his career outside Venice, a public servant who preferred literature to politics, a prose theorist whose models were Florentine rather than Venetian. Yet his Prose della volgar lingua influenced every major Italian writer of the 16th century, his poetry set the standard for Petrarchan imitation, and his official history of Venice — however imperfect — was the most authoritative account of the Republic’s most dangerous war. He represents the cosmopolitan, literary face of the Venetian Renaissance, complementing the more civic-minded humanists like Francesco Barbaro and Bernardo Giustiniani whom Lane discusses as typical of an earlier generation (p. 240).
