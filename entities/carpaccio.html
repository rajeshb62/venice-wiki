Carpaccio

Vittore Carpaccio (c.1465–1525/26) — Vittore Scarpazza was his original name, Venetianised to Carpaccio — was, after the Bellini, the most celebrated narrative painter of late fifteenth-century Venice. Berenson numbers him alongside Giovanni and Gentile Bellini and Cima da Conegliano as one of the painters who met with the greatest success in solving the problems of narrative painting at the end of the century (Berenson, Section V, lines 935–939). He was employed alongside the Bellini and Alvise Vivarini to execute a second rendering of the historical subjects in the Hall of the Great Council of the Doge’s Palace (Section V, lines 1061–1064) — work now destroyed in the fire of 1577, making the surviving confraternity cycles the only evidence for this lost phase of his career.

Formation and Milieu

Carpaccio’s training is not firmly documented, but his work shows the influence of Gentile Bellini — the official state painter responsible for the historical cycles in the Ducal Palace — and, to a lesser degree, of Antonello da Messina, whose visit to Venice in the 1470s introduced a northern European precision of detail and atmospheric depth that Carpaccio absorbed and made his own. He may also have encountered the work of the Flemish masters through the Bruges and Antwerp trade connections: the flower-pots, the brocades, the glinting metalwork, the light through leaded windows that recur throughout his genre scenes have the quality of Flemish panel painting translated into a Venetian idiom.

His working life coincided with the apogee of the Venetian scuole (confraternity) as patrons of painting. The Scuole Grandi and the smaller scuole piccole were in competition with each other to decorate their alberghi (lodge rooms) and sale capitolari (chapter halls) with narrative cycles glorifying their patron saints. This competition created a market for large-scale multi-panel narrative programs that Carpaccio was uniquely suited to fill.

Pageant Pictures

Berenson’s Section V (Pageant Pictures) characterises Carpaccio as a pleasure-loving artist whose work responded to the Venetian love of processions and civic ceremony. His narrative cycles depict the great cortei (processions) that were as central to Venetian state functions as the High Mass — processions on land and water, with the Doge and Senators arrayed in prescribed gorgeous costumes against the fairy-like architecture of the Piazza and canals (Berenson, lines 977–999).

In Carpaccio’s St. Ursula series, the saint’s departure from Brittany and her arrival in Cologne become water-borne pageants in which the ships are Venetian galleys, the harbours are the Venetian bacino, and the ambassadors are dressed in the robes of Venetian senators (lines 998–1005). The viewer is not looking at a British saint in the fifth century: he is looking at Venice in the 1490s, its waterways, its flags, its institutional ceremonies, its light. This is the central paradox of Carpaccio’s sacred narratives — they are documentary records of Venetian civic life in the guise of hagiography.

Confraternity Commissions

Section VI (Painting and the Confraternities) describes how the Scuole Grandi, having seen the pageant pictures in the Doge’s Palace, engaged the same masters to decorate their meeting-rooms. The Scuola di San Giorgio degli Schiavoni (the confraternity of Dalmatian merchants resident in Venice), the Scuola di Sant’Orsola (now the Gallerie dell’Accademia), and the Scuola di Santo Stefano all employed Carpaccio (Berenson, lines 1112–1116).

These confraternity works are of peculiar importance for two reasons. First, they are all that remain to illuminate the style of the lost Doge’s Palace murals destroyed in the fire of 1577 (lines 1117–1121). Second, they mark a transition to a more intimate, domestic art: as the scuole commissioned pictures to glorify their patron saints, the high ceremonial style relaxed and elements taken directly from life — the objects, furniture, light conditions, and street scenes of everyday Venice — were introduced as settings for sacred narratives (lines 1124–1133).

The Scuola di San Giorgio degli Schiavoni cycle (still in its original location) is the most intimate of these: a series of panels depicting episodes from the lives of Saints George, Tryphon, and Jerome, set in an imagined Levantine world that nevertheless reads as Venetian. The San Giorgio e il drago (St. George and the Dragon) is famous for the grisly precision of the bones and body parts scattered around the dragon’s lair — a detail that is simultaneously macabre and documentary.

Genre and Domesticity

For Berenson, Carpaccio’s true genius lies in genre — the painting of ordinary life for its own sake, a century before genre painting became a recognized category. While he loved cortei (processions) and civic ceremony, he loved homelier scenes as well.

His Dream of St. Ursula (Accademia, Venice) is less a saint’s story than the picture of a young girl asleep in a room filled with quiet morning light — the flower-pots in the window, the writing-table, the cupboards, the books, and the lamp all painted for their own sake, as objects worth seeing regardless of their sacred function (Berenson, lines 1170–1182). In King Maure Dismissing the Ambassadors (Ursula cycle), the most striking features are neither king nor ambassadors but the light streaming through a side door and a poor scrivan (clerk) labouring at his task in the corner (lines 1183–1190). The St. Jerome in His Study at the Scuola di San Giorgio degli Schiavoni shows a Venetian humanist scholar in his comfortable, well-lit library, surrounded by books and bric-a-brac, with nothing to speak of a life of self-denial — it is a portrait of the Venetian intellectual as comfortable citizen (lines 1191–1199).

Even the Presentation of the Virgin, which offered a splendid opportunity for pageantry, Carpaccio turned into the picture of a simple girl going to her first communion — concentrating not on the Temple’s architectural grandeur but on the small figure ascending the steps and the watchful parents below (lines 1200–1208). Berenson concludes: “Carpaccio’s quality is the quality of a painter of genre, of which he was the earliest Italian master, dealing with its subject for the sake of pictorial capacities and effects of colour, light, and shade” (lines 1209–1223).

Venice as Subject: The Documentary Value

Carpaccio’s paintings are primary sources for the material culture of late fifteenth-century Venice. The Procession in the Piazza San Marco (Gentile Bellini’s version, but analogous in character to Carpaccio’s scenes) and the Miracle of the Relic record the Piazza before the Renaissance rebuilding changed its appearance. Carpaccio’s vedute (views) — embedded in his narrative cycles as settings — show the gondolas, the warehouse facades, the dress of merchants and clergy, the signs above shops, the types of rigging on the vessels in the harbour. A historian of material culture can extract from the Ursula cycle or the San Giorgio cycle a detailed picture of what Venice looked like, how it sounded, and how its social hierarchies were expressed in clothing and gesture.

This documentary quality was deliberate. Carpaccio’s patrons — the Dalmatian merchants of the Schiavoni, the modest fratelli of Sant’Orsola — wanted to see their own city, their own world, rendered beautiful and permanent by paint. They were not patrons who wished to ascend to a Platonic heaven of abstract forms; they wished to record and celebrate the specific, located, commercial, festive Venice they inhabited.

Style and Legacy

Berenson describes Carpaccio’s art as distinguished by brilliancy, delightful fancy, love of colour, and gaiety of humour — qualities that would have fitted him admirably for pittura da cavalletto (easel painting), though he painted few independent canvases. His sharp detail, bright palette, and narrative clarity make his paintings documents of everyday Venetian life as much as sacred histories.

His legacy passes through Giorgione, identified by Berenson as the follower of both Carpaccio and Giovanni Bellini, who combined “the refined feeling and poetry of Bellini with Carpaccio’s gaiety and love of beauty and colour” (lines 1330–1335). Through Giorgione’s softening of form and deepening of atmospheric unity, Carpaccio’s bright, documentary surface gave way to the more ambiguous, poetic mode of the High Renaissance — a transition as important for the history of Venetian painting as the shift from Gentile to Giovanni Bellini a generation earlier.

The principal surviving cycles — the Accademia (Ursula cycle and others), the Scuola di San Giorgio degli Schiavoni (intact in its original location), and additional works in the Museo Correr and the Uffizi — remain among the most rewarding bodies of narrative painting in European art.
