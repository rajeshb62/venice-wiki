Constitutional Development

Venice’s constitution evolved over half a millennium from a Byzantine-style dukedom into a tightly controlled oligarchic republic. Unlike most Italian city-states, which oscillated between communal liberty and one-man signoria, Venice maintained republican institutions until 1797, adapting its governing councils to meet successive crises while gradually concentrating power in a hereditary nobility.

From Dukedom to Commune: The Early Centuries

In the 9th through 11th centuries, the doge was a monarch of virtually unlimited power. Doges of the Candiano family battled for control of the Adriatic and tried to make the lagoons an hereditary dukedom. Pietro IV Candiano put away his first wife to marry the sister of the Marquis of Tuscany, brought foreign soldiers to Venice, and looked to the German emperor for support. When rivals stormed the ducal palace in 976, the fire spread through the city, destroying San Marco. The doge was killed along with his infant son (Lane, pp. 107-109).

The murderous feuds of the 10th and 11th centuries — the Caloprini versus the Morosini, the overthrow of the Orseolo dynasty — showed that Venice suffered the same factional violence as other Italian cities. The orderly subordination of individual ambition for which Venice was later admired was an acquired characteristic, not an inheritance of original virtue (Lane, pp. 108-110).

A beginning of hemming in the doge with advisers appeared after the overthrow of the Orseolo in 1032. Two ducal councillors were elected to prevent any attempt at hereditary succession. Over the next two centuries, the doge’s powers were gradually restricted by an expanding set of councils representing the wealthier families (Lane, pp. 110-111).

The Commune and the Great Council: The Beginning of Oligarchy

By the late 12th century, Venice had become a commune — a self-governing city-state in which power was exercised by a Consilium Sapientium (Council of the Wise) that later evolved into the Great Council. The doge retained executive authority but was bound to act with the consent of his councillors and the greater council.

The decisive turning point was the Serrata of the Great Council in 1297 — the “locking” or “closing” that transformed Venice from a broadly-based commune into a hereditary oligarchy. But the Serrata was initially an enlargement, not an exclusion (Lane, pp. 113-114).

Before 1297, membership in the Great Council was determined by annual elections. There was no formal barrier to entry, but in practice the wealthier families dominated the nominating committees. The Council was a self-perpetuating body of several hundred men, but it was not yet a closed caste.

In 1297, Doge Pietro Gradenigo pushed through a reform that changed everything. The new law removed all limitations on the size of the Council and provided that all those who were already members, or had been members during the last four years, were to be permanent members — approved by as few as twelve votes in the Council of Forty. Lane notes: “No wonder that this proposal, giving practical assurance that those already in would continue to be members, gained a sufficient number of the members’ votes!” (Lane, p. 113).

At the same time, the new law provided that other persons could be proposed for membership by a nominating committee of three, subject to the same lenient approval threshold. Under this procedure, “a number of commoners of old Venetian families were made permanent members of the Council,” and “a dozen or so families from Beyond-the-Sea, refugees after the fall of Acre in 1291” were also admitted. The Council’s membership more than doubled, rising to over 1,100 (Lane, p. 113).

Gradenigo’s motive was political. He had been elected over Giacomo Tiepolo, the popular candidate backed by the populace. The commoners who had shouted for Tiepolo may have resented Gradenigo, but the reform was not directed against them as a class. It was designed to strengthen the Great Council’s authority and enable it to eclipse the General Assembly — the popular gathering that had once elected doges. The reform was put through in the middle of the Second Genoese War, just after the costly defeat at Curzola (1298), when the Republic needed stable government. The expansion diluted the power of any single faction and gave the Council a legitimacy that no popular assembly could challenge.

But the openness was short-lived. Within a few years, curbs were imposed on new admissions. The voting threshold in the Council of Forty was raised: first to a majority, then to 25 votes, then to 30. These restrictions were “climaxed by a clear declaration in 1323 that, in order to be a member of the Great Council, a man must show that he had an ancestor who had held high posts in the Commune” (Lane, p. 114). By that date, membership in the Great Council had become permanent and hereditary and a prerequisite for election to any other council or magistracy.

The old line between nobles and commoners disappeared. Henceforth, membership in the Great Council was nobility, and nobility was hereditary. Those whose names were inscribed in the Golden Book were the only citizens eligible for political office. The Serrata, which began as an enlargement to stabilise the Republic, had become a closed hereditary caste.

The enlargement of the Great Council and the addition of the Council of Ten rounded out the aristocratic structure. They provided steadiness, fast action in emergencies, and involvement of the whole aristocracy in deliberations leading to important decisions. Compared to most cities, there was a general feeling of solidarity and loyalty among the Venetian nobility (Lane, pp. 137-138).

The Council of Ten and Police Powers

The Conspiracy of Bajamonte Tiepolo in 1310 — an attempted coup by disgruntled nobles supported by some of the common people — prompted the creation of a temporary Council of Ten. It proved so useful that it became permanent. Its three chairmen (Capi) each held office for only one month; membership was for one year, and no two members could be of the same family. Initially tasked with overseeing the sentences against the exiles, the Ten eventually tracked and eliminated Venetian fuorusciti by a combination of leniency and lethal efficiency (Lane, pp. 136-137).

Gradually, the Council of Ten built a permanent dual role. First, it was small enough that the doge and his inner council could bring to it matters requiring urgency and secrecy. Second, it took the initiative in internal police: suppressing not only armed insurrection but also any noble acting above the law, and any attempt to organize a faction or party (Lane, pp. 137-138). The Ten permitted no organized opposition; indeed, any beginning of organized parties, even by those in power, would have been judged a corruption of public spirit.

The 16th-Century Constitution Perfected

By the 16th century, Venice’s constitution had reached its final form. The Great Council (Maggior Consiglio), comprising all adult male nobles (about 2,000-2,500), was the sovereign body. It elected all magistrates, including the Senate, the Council of Ten, and the doge. The Senate (Pregadi) managed foreign policy, finance, and trade. The Council of Ten handled state security, censorship, and political policing. The Collegio (the doge plus six councillors and the three heads of the Quarantia) functioned as a cabinet. And the doge, once a near-monarch, was hedged with so many restrictions that he could do nothing without at least one of his councils (Lane, pp. 271-273).

The nobility — about six to seven percent of the city’s population — exercised sovereign power. In 1423, the Great Council declared its own decrees valid without popular approval; the ceremony of presenting the new doge to the people was retained but empty of meaning. In 1462, the doge’s oath was altered to remove all references to a “Commune of the Venetians.” The government became known as La Serenissima (Lane, pp. 271-273).

The nobility became more exclusive over time. There were no new admissions like those of 1381. A 1403 proposal to admit a new family whenever one of the old families died out was killed by the Ducal Council. Nobles of subject cities like Verona were given no part in Venetian government (Lane, pp. 272-273). The constitution, unwritten in any single document, consisted of a style and tradition of political behavior based on custom as much as on particular laws. Its core principles were checks on irresponsible personal power, short terms of office, delegation of responsibilities to committees rather than individuals, and the aristocratic principle that power belonged by right to the well-born (Lane, pp. 271-272).

Venice’s republican institutions were unique in 16th-century Europe. Nearly everywhere else, republican principles were abandoned. Venice alone survived with independence while perpetuating republican government, admired for domestic peace, impartial justice, and a system of public health — but the price was a rigid hereditary oligarchy that could not adapt to the scale of modern state power (Lane, pp. 271-273).

The Myth of Venice as Political Model

The Venetian constitution fascinated European observers. In an age of absolute monarchy, Venice demonstrated that a republic could preserve its independence, maintain internal peace, and command the respect of great powers. The “Myth of Venice” — the belief that Venetian institutions were uniquely perfect and incorruptible — was actively cultivated by the Republic’s own historians and propagandists.

In reality, the constitution was both less virtuous and more adaptive than the myth allowed. The myth of freedom from factions ignored the murderous feuds of the 9th-11th centuries. The myth of a perfectly balanced constitution (mixing monarchic, aristocratic, and democratic elements) obscured the fact that real power was firmly in the hands of a hereditary caste. Yet the myth itself was a political reality: it gave the Venetian nobility a sense of collective purpose and civic duty that helped maintain the Republic’s stability (Lane, pp. 107-108).

The Venetian system of election by secret ballot, short terms of office, and collective decision-making was genuinely innovative. Venetian ambassadors developed the art of diplomatic reporting to a level unmatched in Europe. The Republic’s Board of Health, its public grain supply, and its system of poor relief were also widely admired. Lane notes that Venice “aroused admiration for many political and administrative achievements” and that “its populace was relatively well-fed and creative” (Lane, p. 271).
