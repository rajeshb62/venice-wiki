A Proletariat Among Seamen

Venice’s maritime empire was built on the labour of its seamen, but by the 14th century the conditions of oarsmen on the galleys had deteriorated to the point that Lane speaks of a “proletariat among seamen” — a class of impoverished, debt-ridden rowers who were increasingly coerced into service. The transformation of the Venetian seafaring population from a reserve of skilled, independent mariners into a labour force of semi-impressed paupers was one of the most significant social changes of the late medieval period.

Free Rowers and Their Wages

In the 13th century, Venetian seamen were recruited freely, attracted by wages, cargo privileges, and the prospect of adventure. The maritime code of 1255 regulated contracts between shipmasters and crews, specifying that seamen were to be paid by the voyage.

By the 14th century, the system had changed. Oarsmen (galeotti) were paid by the day from departure to return, not by the voyage. The daily wage was only a part of what a seaman expected to gain: on commercial voyages, all had rights to carry freight-free some merchandise for their own account — a form of duty-free trading privilege that could be more valuable than wages. On military expeditions, their readiness to sign on was stimulated by hopes of booty (Lane, pp. 168-169).

A typical oarsman earned about 10 lire per month in the mid-14th century, but much of this was paid in advance — often three or four months’ wages — as a signing bonus. This advance system was designed to attract recruits in a competitive labour market, but it also trapped seamen in debt. If a man failed to report for a voyage, he could not repay the advance and faced imprisonment (Lane, pp. 168-170).

The Free Row Controversy

Venice never adopted large-scale use of convict rowers until after Lepanto (1571). For most of the 14th-16th centuries, the galley fleet was rowed by free men who had voluntarily signed on. In principle, the galeotto was a free labourer who had accepted an advance of wages in return for his services.

In practice, the line between free and forced labour was blurred. Those who accepted advances and then failed to report were pursued by the police of the Nightwatch. In 1322, the police were offered 2 grossi for each galeotto they could round up. A law of 1329 required that those jumping ship should be sent to jail until they paid what they owed. When the jails filled with men who had no money, the Great Council voted to release them into the custody of the galley masters to work off their debts (Lane, pp. 169-170).

Imprisonment for debt was the usual way of forcing collection, and service on the galleys was one way of getting out of debtors’ prison. So many people avoided paying debts by taking service on a galley (thus acquiring immunity from arrest) that a law of 1312 limited this privilege to those whose debts did not exceed 20 lire. The number imprisoned in the 1330s and then put to work as debt slaves showed that many of Venice’s seamen were in dire poverty (Lane, p. 169).

Working Conditions on the Galleys

Life on a Venetian galley was harsh. The standard ration was ship’s biscuit (18 oz per man per day), wine, and bean soup — supplemented on alternate days with salt pork, cheese, and sardines. By the mid-14th century, the salt pork had disappeared from the ration. When the admiral Niccolo Pisani sponsored a motion requiring galley masters to provide meat three days a week, the Senate voted it down. Venice entered the Third Genoese War with its seamen depleted and discontented (Lane, p. 170).

The shortage of maritime labour was accentuated by the Black Death of 1348, which killed about half of Venice’s population. After the plague, fugitives from justice were invited back if they would work on the galleys, and the number of men required per ton on round ships was reduced to ease the dearth of seamen (Lane, p. 170).

The food deterioration was part of a broader decline in the status of seamen. The 13th-century galeotto had been a respectable craftsman; the 14th-century galeotto was increasingly a man with no other option. There were revolts of seamen in many Mediterranean ports in the 14th century — at Genoa in 1339, at Salonica in 1345, at Barcelona in 1391 — reflecting the impoverishment and loss of status of a part of the seafaring population across the Mediterranean (Lane, pp. 169-170).

Crewmen’s Own Venture: Duty-Free Trade

The most important economic privilege of the Venetian seaman was the right to carry a certain amount of freight-free merchandise for his own account. On commercial galleys, each crewman — from the captain down to the lowest oarsman — was allowed a specified volume of cargo space free of freight charges. This was called the portata or carato privilege.

For skilled sailors, the cargo privilege could be worth far more than their wages. Navigators and helmsmen (armirai and omo di conseio) — experts in dead-reckoning and marine charts — were paid three times as much as a galeotto and had more extended cargo privileges. These skilled professionals were far from being proletarians; Lane notes that “sailors were the first professional group to use mathematics in their everyday work” and they commanded high value in the labour market (Lane, p. 169).

For ordinary rowers, however, the cargo privilege was limited and often mortgaged in advance. The advance-payment system meant that much of a rower’s potential earnings — including his trading privilege — were already pledged to creditors before the voyage began. The galeotto thus became a proletarian in the strict sense: a free man who owned no capital and whose labour was his only asset, but even that labour was encumbered by debt.

The Shift to Convict Labour

After the Battle of Lepanto (1571), the naval situation changed dramatically. For a decade after the battle, few new galleys were built, and captured Turkish prisoners of war were used as galley slaves. Then, during the long peace that followed, patrolling galleys were rowed almost entirely by convicts, with only about 50 unchained free men — nearly all sailors or soldiers — to a galley. Only on some of the flagships were all aboard free men (Lane, p. 373).

This marked the final transformation of the Venetian seaman from a free craftsman-tradesman into a member of a coerced labour force. The proletarianization of the oarsmen was a symptom of deeper changes: the decline of Venetian maritime supremacy, the shift of noble investment from shipping to land, and the growing difficulty of recruiting free Venetian oarsmen in a city whose economy was increasingly oriented to industry and finance rather than the sea.
