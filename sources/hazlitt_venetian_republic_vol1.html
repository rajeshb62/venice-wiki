Hazlitt — The Venetian Republic, Vol. I (1900)

Full Title: The Venetian Republic: Its Rise, Its Growth, and Its Fall
Author: William Carew Hazlitt (1834–1913)
Publisher: A. & C. Black, London, 1900
Source: Anna’s Archive / Harvard College Library scan (OCLC 7075165). 850 page-image text files in sources/32044105539571/.
Line Count: ~2,053,000 characters across 850 pages.

This is the first volume of Hazlitt’s comprehensive history of Venice. It traces the Republic from its origins in the lagoon settlements (409 AD) through the consolidation of its constitution, its rise as a Mediterranean maritime power, the Crusades, the struggle with Genoa, the War of Chioggia, and the acquisition of a mainland empire — ending at 1431 with the reign of Doge Francesco Foscari and the acquisition of Saloniki.

The work draws heavily on Romanin’s Storia Documentata di Venezia and the Calendar of Venetian State Papers, as well as the diaries of Marino Sanuto and other contemporary sources. Hazlitt frames Venice’s political structure as “perfectly unparalleled” and treats its 14-century lifespan as a dramatic arc from rise to zenith to dissolution.

The 15th and 16th centuries (the period of Venice’s peak power and gradual decline) are covered in the subsequent volumes, which are not present in this archive.

Contents

CHAPTER I — A.D. 409–654 (p. 1)

Venetia Princeps — Successive rulers of the mainland. The earliest lagoon settlements, the formation of the Venetian people from refugees fleeing barbarian invasions, and the first rudimentary constitutions.

CHAPTER II — A.D. 654–787 (p. 17)

Tribunitial government abolished — establishment of the dogeship — vicissitudes of the office. Military constitution of Venice, the first Italian treaty, and the Republic’s first belligerent actions.

CHAPTER III — A.D. 787–809 (p. 31)

Internal discord — balancing policy between the Greeks and Franks — defeat of the Frankish forces in 809. The rivalry of Heraclia and Malamocco.

CHAPTER IV — A.D. 809–864 (p. 46)

The Badoer dynasty — commencement of architectural improvements — continuance of civil war — growth of power. A doge seeks the cloister.

CHAPTER V — A.D. 864–912 (p. 59)

Schism with the Holy See — financial schemes — fall of a doge in battle — rise of the House of Sanudo — first continental acquisition at Terra Firma — the Huns again in Italy.

CHAPTER VI — A.D. 912–991 (p. 73)

Brides of St. Mark — fall of the Sanudo dynasty — succession of the Orseoli — a doge turns monk.

CHAPTER VII — A.D. 991–1084 (p. 103)

The Orseolo dynasty — submission of Dalmatia — constitutional reforms — the Northmen.

CHAPTER VIII — A.D. 1084–1128 (p. 141)

The Faliero and Michieli dynasties — the Crusades — Venetian accessions in the Levant — the doge at Jerusalem.

CHAPTER IX — A.D. 1128–1172 (p. 176)

Important relations and alliances — farther operations in Sicily and Greece — the Lombard League.

CHAPTER X — A.D. 1173–1201 (p. 205)

Constitutional and official developments — creation of the Great Council — participation in the Fifth Crusade.

CHAPTER XI — A.D. 1202–1205 (p. 246)

Departure for Constantinople — the Fifth Crusade — the voyage to Constantinople — arrival there — trials and perils.

CHAPTER XII — A.D. 1203–1205 (p. 279)

Fall of Constantinople — election of a new emperor — partition of the Empire — the Republic’s large share in the conquest.

CHAPTER XIII — A.D. 1205–1229 (p. 310)

A new era — difficulties regarding possessions — early decline of the Latin power at Constantinople — the Jubilee of 1214 — Venetian colonisation — a startling proposal.

CHAPTER XIV — A.D. 1229–1249 (p. 341)

Increasing political prominence of Venice in European affairs — pawn of the Crown of Thorns — the Republic and the German Empire.

CHAPTER XV — A.D. 1249–1268 (p. 362)

Troubles in the East — hostilities with Genoa — Padua and the Eccelini — legal reforms.

CHAPTER XVI — A.D. 1268–1280 (p. 398)

Institution of the Chancery — festivities at a coronation — declaration of sovereignty over the Adriatic.

CHAPTER XVII — A.D. 1280–1289 (p. 424)

A republican doge elected — formation of a league against Venice — the Sicilian Vespers — the Holy Office at Venice — the first gold ducat.

CHAPTER XVIII — A.D. 1289–1302 (p. 442)

War with Genoa and the Greek emperor — severe reverses of the Republic — Marco Polo — return of peace in 1302.

CHAPTER XIX — A.D. 1286–1309 (p. 474)

Constitutional agitation — Serrar del Gran Consiglio — rupture with the Pope — interdict of 1309.

CHAPTER XX — A.D. 1310–1311 (p. 517)

The Quirini-Tiepolo conspiracy — establishment of a temporary Committee of Public Safety (Council of Ten) — institution of a police.

CHAPTER XXI — A.D. 1311–1342 (p. 537)

Venetian prosperity — relations with European and Asiatic powers — Council of Ten made a permanent assembly — acquisition of Treviso.

CHAPTER XXII — A.D. 1343–1354 (p. 569)

Active military and naval operations — severe struggle with Genoa — Petrarch.

CHAPTER XXIII — A.D. 1354–1355 (p. 592)

Continuation of the Genoese war — story of Marino Faliero.

CHAPTER XXIV — A.D. 1355–1365 (p. 614)

Hostilities with Genoa and Hungary — trouble in Candia (Crete) — a doge with a taste for horses and a dislike to interference.

CHAPTER XXV — A.D. 1365–1378 (p. 643)

Last days of Petrarch — his gift to Venice — growth of a coalition against the Republic between Austria and other powers — Sir John Hawkwood.

CHAPTER XXVI — A.D. 1378–1383 (p. 669)

The War of Chioggia — a fight for life — Venice ultimately triumphant — romantic details.

CHAPTER XXVII — A.D. 1383–1413 (p. 718)

Decline of Genoa — important accessions of Italian territory to Venice — rise of the Osmanli (Ottomans) and of the House of Visconti — great achievements of Carlo Zeno.

CHAPTER XXVIII — A.D. 1414–1431 (p. 785)

The Pacific Doge Mocenigo — death of Carlo Zeno — operations against the Emperor Sigismund — extensive gains of territory in the Levant — acquisition of Saloniki.

END OF VOL. I — Subsequent volumes continue through the 15th, 16th, and 17th centuries to the Republic’s fall in 1797.
